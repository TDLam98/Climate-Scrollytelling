Climate Change by the Numbers

Four questions about warming, CO₂, disasters and forecasts, tested with public data. Scroll down and the charts change as you read.

Scroll ↓

1. Has warming accelerated?

NASA GISTEMP global temperature, 1880–2025

The long climb

Each dot is one year's global temperature compared with the 1951–1980 average. Since 1880 the trend is clearly up. The question is whether it is going up at a steady pace.

Two speeds

A two-segment model finds a break around 1975 (95% interval 1965 to 1985). Before it, warming was about 0.04 °C per decade. After it, about 0.21 °C per decade, a difference of 0.17 (95% CI 0.13 to 0.22).

The model with a break fits much better than a straight line (BIC −607 vs. −470).

2. How are CO₂ and temperature related?

NOAA Mauna Loa CO₂ and GISTEMP, 1959–2025

A near-perfect line

Plot CO₂ against temperature and the points line up almost perfectly (r = 0.97). But that is mostly because both simply rise over time, so it proves little by itself.

Year to year, the link is modest

Looking at yearly changes removes most of the shared climb. The link is still there, but weaker: r = 0.32, or 0.39 once the leftover time trend is removed. That is about 15% of the year-to-year variation.

This is association, not proof of cause. El Niño years raise both CO₂ growth and temperature.

3. Are disasters becoming more frequent?

EM-DAT climate-related events, 2000–2025

About 300 to 400 a year

EM-DAT records roughly 300–400 climate-related disasters each year since 2000. The trend is up about 3.9% per decade, but the 95% interval (−1.6% to +9.8%) includes zero, so we cannot call it a clear rise.

Only storms stand out

By type, only storms show a clear increase (+10.8% per decade, 95% CI 1.6 to 20.9), and that is one result out of five, so it could be chance.

Reported damages rose about 52% per decade, but only about a third of events report a damage value, so better reporting may explain part of it.

4. Do forecasts and climate models hold up?

Backtest and IPCC AR6 / CMIP6 scenarios

Same start, different futures

A simple trend fitted to 1970–2005 predicted 2006–2025 within about 0.10 °C on average, three times better than assuming warming stopped. Climate-model scenarios agree for now and fan out after about 2030, depending on emissions.

Zooming in on today

Observed temperatures, shifted to the IPCC's 1850–1900 baseline, fell inside the 5–95% scenario ranges in 73–82% of years since 2015, about 0.02–0.05 °C above the scenario averages.

The three scenarios are nearly identical so far, so today's data cannot show which one we are on.

The big picture

The temperature signal is clear. The disaster-record signal is not.

  • Warming sped up in the mid-1970s, from about 0.04 to 0.21 °C per decade.
  • CO₂ growth and temperature change are modestly linked year to year.
  • Disaster counts show no clear overall rise since 2000, and reporting limits what we can say.
  • Simple trend models forecast well, and observed warming tracks the climate-model scenarios.
Data: NASA GISS GISTEMP v4; NOAA Global Monitoring Laboratory (Mauna Loa CO₂); EM-DAT, CRED/UCLouvain; IPCC AR6 WGI Figure SPM.8 via CEDA. Limits: association is not causation; EM-DAT reporting changes over time; three of five scenarios shown.
